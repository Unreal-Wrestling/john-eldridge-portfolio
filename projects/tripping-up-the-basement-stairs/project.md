Tripping up the Basement Stairs before Dawn is a collection of songs
and poetry by my father, John Seymour Eldridge. He died before it was
published. I did the cover design concept and typography, set the
200-page interior, and prepared the production files Balboa Press
needed to print and distribute it.

Four of us worked on the book, and the division matters. Leah
Griffiths, a Washington-based illustrator, drew the cover artwork.
Richard Gerber, a guitarist who played with Merrilee Rush and Paul
Revere and the Raiders, took the author photograph. Bonnie Masterjohn
made the illustrations that run inside the poem "A Dozen
Indiscretions." Mine is the typography, the layout, the interior
typesetting and the production — not the drawings.

The title carries its own picture: someone moving through a dark
transitional space before the world is up. The job was to hold that
without illustrating it flatly, and to do it on a self-publishing
imprint's fixed templates, in black and white, on creme stock, at 5.5
by 8.5 inches. No spot colour, no foil, no unusual trim. Whatever the
cover was going to do, it had to do in greyscale.

## Reference

I started from photographs. One in particular set the framing — a
derelict brick stairwell climbing to a doorway blown out with light,
leaves collected on the treads. It has the quality the title needs,
which is that the light is at the top and you are not there yet.

The second photograph in this section is not reference for the
illustration. It is Richard Gerber's portrait of my father, and it
runs on the back cover. I mention it here because it is the only warm
image in the entire book, and knowing it was coming changed what the
front could be. With a colour photograph of the author on the back,
the front was free to stay cold and empty.

## Illustration Studies

Before commissioning anything I built a comp to agree the composition,
using a watermarked stock photograph as a stand-in. That is all that
comp is — a placeholder proving the framing, not a design. Using the
photo itself was never an option, and its watermark is a useful
reminder of why the drawing had to be commissioned.

[[cover-comp.jpg]]

Griffiths' studies are below, and they circle two different pictures.
Several look straight up a flight toward daylight, following the
reference photograph. Others look at an arched opening from inside a
dark room, where the stair is something you see rather than something
you are on. One study puts a small figure at the head of the stairs.

The arch won, and the figure came out. Looking up the flight puts the
reader on the staircase, which sounds right for the title and is
actually too much — it fixes the moment and closes the metaphor. The
arch keeps the reader in the dark room looking at a way out. And a
figure, once you add one, becomes the subject; the poems are the
subject. What is left is a stone opening, light pooling on the floor,
and the stair rising into shadow.

## Cover Design

The typography is one classical serif, set light and generously
spaced, and no script anywhere. The title steps down the left in five
short lines that follow the stair's own diagonal, so the type sits in
the drawing's dark side rather than on top of its lit side, and the
author's name closes the sequence at the bottom in the same face at a
heavier weight. White type on a black field, nothing else on the
front — with only greyscale available, the contrast between the type
and the charcoal is the entire design, and adding a second typeface
would have spent contrast the drawing needed.

I built two production files. The perfect-bound softcover wraps front,
.458-inch spine and back as one piece, with the author photograph,
biography, Balboa mark and barcode on the back. The dust jacket
extends the same artwork to 8.75 inches with 3.25-inch flaps. Both are
shown here as the printer's templates, so the trim, bleed and wrap
zones are visible, along with the grey boxes marking where the press
drops its own caption, logo and barcode. The contributor biographies
run down the back flap.

## Typesetting & Interior

Inside, the script finally appears, and only for poem titles — the
handwritten note against Tisa Pro for the verse. My father's
manuscripts were handwritten, and a script title is the one place that
can show without becoming a costume.

The device that does the most work is the ghost. Each poem's title
repeats at the head of the page, enormous and pale, bleeding off the
top edge. It gives a page that may hold only twenty words a piece of
structure to sit under, so short poems do not float in a field of
white, and it costs nothing on a black-and-white press.

The verse itself is stepped across the measure rather than set flush,
because my father wrote in indented, cascading lines and those breaks
are part of the poem. Ranging everything left would have been tidier
and would have quietly rewritten him.

I set the interior in InDesign at the Balboa trim, 200 pages, black
and white on creme, and delivered a print-ready PDF with separate
softcover and dust jacket cover files to the press's specification.

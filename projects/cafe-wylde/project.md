Cafe Wylde is a vegan restaurant in Everett, Washington. A friend was
opening the place and asked me for a logo. The name was already
decided and it pointed hard in one direction — untamed, overgrown, a
little feral — and the obvious job was to draw that.

The name also splits in a useful way. "Cafe" is the plain half that
tells you what the business is; "Wylde" is the half doing the
promising. So almost every round I built paired two different
typefaces against each other, testing which half should carry the
personality and how far it could go before the other half stopped
being readable. I pulled down eighteen display faces to work with —
Blowbrush, Docktrin, The Dead Saloon, Vanilla Whale, CocoBiker and
the rest — and started setting pairs.

The first four rounds chased the name. Brush-marker scrawls, angular
art-deco caps, inline western slabs, and by round four a heavy
circus-poster face with rough inked texture and both words
underlined. On the sheet they looked good. As a restaurant they were
wrong, and it took putting four rounds on the wall to see why: every
one of them read as a bar, a barbecue pit, or a tattoo shop. They
promised something loud and probably fried. This was a place serving
vegetables, and the logo was setting an expectation the kitchen was
never going to meet.

So round six went the other way. I threw out the display faces and
set the name in classical high-contrast Roman serifs — the sort of
letterform you find on a wine label or a good menu — and kept one
squared bold setting on the sheet as a control, to be sure I was
reacting to the work rather than to boredom. The serif was better
immediately, and the reason is the word itself. Set in a display
face, "Wylde" is a genre signal. Set in a Didone, it reads as a
surname. It stops sounding like a theme and starts sounding like a
proprietor, which is what makes a restaurant feel like a restaurant
and not a concept.

Rounds eight and ten narrowed the mechanics. One condensed
high-contrast serif for both words rather than a pair. Small caps
against full caps. How much bigger "Wylde" should sit against
"CAFE" — and eventually, not bigger at all: both words set as
equal-weight caps, so neither half of the name outranks the other
and the lockup reads as one line rather than a hierarchy.

That left the original problem unsolved. The type was disciplined
now, and nothing in it was wild. Round twelve resolved it by
splitting the job: "Organic Cuisine" in a formal italic script under
the caps to do the explaining, and a line-art vine of flowering
stems climbing the right side to carry the growth. Handing the
wildness to an illustration is what let the type stay quiet, and the
vine does structural work too — two lines of caps make a hard
rectangle, and the stems break the corner so the mark has an
irregular silhouette instead of sitting in a block. I tested it
reduced on the same sheet, because the vine has the thinnest strokes
in the drawing and they are the first thing to close up on a
business card.

Twelve rounds in twelve days, May 22 to June 2, 2016, delivered as
vector AI and EPS with a print PDF and a web PNG.
